## Working Papers

**Big Push Policies and Network Externalities: Insights from Quebec's EV Fast-Charging Market**<br>
2nd Place, Thesis in 5 Minutes, Climate Positive Energy

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>Industries with strong complementarities and network externalities may exhibit multiple equilibria. I examine whether government provision of network infrastructure can remedy the resulting inefficiency. Using vehicle registration and charging transaction data from Québec, I estimate a structural model of EV demand in which consumers value the charging network through the origin–destination trips it supports. I decompose the effect of a large government network deployment—a Big Push—into a coordination component, shifting the market from the low- to the high-adoption equilibrium, and an externality component, capturing stations that remain privately unprofitable even under full coordination. The Big Push raises EV adoption by 67%, with coordination contributing 31% and externalities a further 27%. It expands the network from 249 to 921 chargers, crowding out private investment in the core while crowding it in at the periphery, and generates $484 million in consumer value and $88 million in climate benefits for $22 million of government investment. This paper provides empirical evidence that direct government provision of network infrastructure can resolve coordination failures.</p>
</details>

**Safer, Faster, Farther: Evidence from Montreal's Bike Lane Network**

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>The installation of bike lanes at the cost of traffic lanes and parking has become common practice in major American cities. Proponents argue that these measures raise cycling ridership, but evidence remains scarce. I address this question using the rollout of Montreal's Bike Network as a natural experiment. I combine data on deployed bike corridors with origin-destination data from the city-owned bike-sharing system. I first exploit the phased deployment of the city's bike lane network and find that protected bike lanes reduce collisions. I then estimate a difference-in-differences model using a control corridor on the other side of Mount Royal. I find that cycling speed increases by 7% and trip distance by 8%, contributing to a 32% increase in ridership. I find no evidence that it reflects business stealing from nearby bike lanes. I also find no significant effect on subway ridership, suggesting that the increase in cycling trips is primarily additional rather than a substitution away from public transit.</p>
</details>

**The Impact of On-street Charging Stations and Charging Congestion on EV Adoption**<br>
with Katalin Springel and Eric Nussbaum

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>The transition to electric vehicles (EVs) is a central component of decarbonization plans around the world. However, in many large cities, millions of people live in multi-unit buildings without a dedicated parking space where they can install a private EV charger. In these contexts, expanding access to on-street charging near residents' homes is an important policy objective. However, these investments are costly and their benefits remain uncertain. To shed light on this question, we study the deployment of Montreal's public on-street charging network, the largest such network in North America. We use data on EV ownership and charging-station locations, together with transaction-level charging usage. Our analysis is highly granular, allowing us to compare changes in EV adoption across street blocks. To address endogenous charger placement, we construct an instrument using pre-existing underground transformers, proximity to which substantially reduces station installation costs. Our preliminary results show an economically meaningful and statistically significant effect of the local charging network on EV adoption. Our preferred estimate comes from our heterogeneous-effects specification: adding a charging station in a neighborhood with a low prevalence of detached housing, where residents are therefore likely to have limited access to private parking, increases EV adoption by 9%. This estimate implies a back-of-the-envelope abatement cost of approximately $104 per tonne of avoided CO<sub>2</sub>. An additional charger may also reduce congestion at nearby stations, while the EVs it induces can create additional congestion. The reduced-form adoption estimate captures the net equilibrium effect of these responses but does not separate the direct effect of additional charging capacity from the congestion response. To quantify these channels, we are developing a structural model of EV choice as a function of charging-station availability.</p>
</details>

## Work in Progress

**The Impact of Violent Crimes on Commuting: Evidence from Toronto**<br>
with William Arbour and Émile Gendreau Côté

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>The relationship between public safety and urban transportation systems has emerged as a critical policy concern in recent decades. It remains unclear whether heightened concern about crime affects subway ridership or alternative modes such as car hailing. That is the objective of this paper. Using a measure of transit-crime exposure, we find a substantial increase in car hailing usage, but it is concentrated on the day of the event. As this increase likely reflects mechanical disruptions, such as line closures, rather than behavioral responses, we remove the day of the event from the analysis and find a small negative effect of subway crime on car hailing demand, underlining the complementarity between the subway and car hailing. We explore this further, as the average may mask offsetting responses driven by the interaction between private cars, car hailing, and the subway at different times of the week. At the 1-, 7-, and 14-day horizons, transit-crime exposure significantly decreases car hailing usage on weekends (Saturday–Sunday), with weak evidence of increases on weekdays, especially Fridays. This pattern strengthens with window length, consistent with the subway and car hailing acting as complements on weekdays (riders substitute the affected segment with a car hailing trip) and as substitutes on weekends (riders forgo the subway entirely, eliminating the complementary car hailing leg). Finally, we analyze whether crimes along the path (not just at endpoints) also affect usage, and whether the response varies by crime severity. Both dimensions show suggestive but inconclusive effects.</p>
</details>
